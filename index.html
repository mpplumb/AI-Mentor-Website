AI, explained without the hype.

Most of what business owners hear about AI is either a sales pitch or a scare story. Here's the plain version — what these systems actually are, what they're genuinely good at, and where they fall over.

The short version

It's a very good pattern-matcher, not a mind.

A large language model has read an enormous amount of text and learned which words tend to follow which. That's it — but it turns out an enormous amount of office work is exactly that: reading something, recognising what kind of thing it is, and writing the appropriate response. That's where the returns come from.

What it doesn't have is judgment, accountability, or any knowledge of your business beyond what you show it. It will answer confidently whether or not it's right. Every useful deployment treats that as a design constraint, not a footnote.

Reality check

What it's good at, and what it isn't.

Genuinely good at

  • Sorting and routingReading an email, form or ticket and deciding where it goes and who handles it.
  • First draftsReplies, summaries, proposals, listings — anything a person reviews before it goes out.
  • Extracting structurePulling dates, amounts and names out of invoices, contracts and PDFs.
  • Answering from your documentsA handbook or catalogue turned into something staff can just ask.
  • Translating formatsNotes into a report, a transcript into action items, a sheet into a summary.

Bad at, or not worth it

  • Anything that must be exactly right, unsupervisedPayroll, tax filings, medical or legal decisions.
  • Arithmetic and reconciliationUse software that calculates; use AI to read the document feeding it.
  • Knowing today's factsWithout a live source it works from memory and will guess.
  • Work you do twice a yearThe automation costs more than the hours it saves.
  • Replacing the person with judgmentIt moves that person up the stack; it doesn't remove them.
Myths

Four things you can stop worrying about.

"We need our own AI model."

Almost nobody does. Training a model from scratch costs millions and the general-purpose ones are better at nearly everything. What you need is good plumbing between an existing model and your own data.

"Our data will be used to train it."

On business plans from the major providers, it generally isn't — but the specifics vary by vendor and plan. That's a question worth asking out loud before anything is connected, and one of the first things I check.

"It'll replace our staff."

In practice it removes the parts of the job nobody wanted — the copying, the sorting, the chasing. The teams getting value from it are the ones where people stayed and stopped doing clerical work.

"It's too early / it's too late."

Neither. The tools are stable enough to build real things on, and the businesses using them well are mostly doing unglamorous, well-scoped work anyone could start this quarter.

Fun facts

Five things about AI worth knowing.

None of these will change your business plan. All of them are true, and they make the technology a lot less mysterious.

01

The field was supposed to take one summer

The term "artificial intelligence" was coined in a 1955 proposal for a workshop at Dartmouth College. The authors asked for two months and ten men, and expected a significant advance. Seventy years later, people are still working on it.

02

"Robot" came from a play

Czech writer Karel Čapek introduced the word in his 1920 play R.U.R. It comes from robota — forced labour. His brother Josef suggested it.

03

People trusted a chatbot in 1966

ELIZA just rephrased your sentences back as questions. Its creator, Joseph Weizenbaum, was unsettled to find his own secretary asking for privacy to talk to it. We still call it the ELIZA effect.

04

Neural networks are older than the transistor

McCulloch and Pitts published the first mathematical model of a neural network in 1943. Bell Labs didn't demonstrate the transistor until 1947.

05

It struggles to spell "strawberry"

Ask a model how many R's are in strawberry and it often gets it wrong — it reads chunks called tokens, not individual letters. Brilliant at drafting your contract, fooled by a spelling-bee question.

Glossary

The words people use at you.

LLM

Large language model

The thing behind ChatGPT and Claude. Reads text, writes text. The engine, not the product.

RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation

Giving the model your own documents to answer from, so it quotes your handbook instead of guessing.

AGENT

Agent

A model that can take actions — send the email, update the record — not only write text. Useful, and worth a human checkpoint.

TOKEN

Token

Roughly three-quarters of a word. It's how usage is billed, which is why long documents cost more to process.

HALLUCINATION

Hallucination

When the model states something false with complete confidence. Managed with grounding and review steps, never fully eliminated.

Principles

Five rules AI Mentor does not bend.

01

Measure before, or don't bother

Every engagement starts with a baseline — how many hours, how many errors, how long the turnaround is today. Without that number, nobody can say later whether it worked, and the project quietly loses its budget.

02

You own everything

Built in your accounts, your infrastructure, your logins. No proprietary platform you have to keep renting to keep the lights on, and no dependency on me a year from now.

03

Teach while building

Your people are in the room while it's built, not handed a finished box. The difference between a system that survives and one that dies is whether somebody inside understands it.

04

A human stays where the risk is

Anything with money, legal exposure or a customer relationship attached gets a review step by design. Automation that removes the last human from a risky decision is how businesses get hurt.

05

Small and shipped beats big and pending

One workflow live in three weeks teaches you more than a six-month roadmap. The first build is chosen for speed to proof, not for how impressive it sounds.

The framework

Every process gets three scores.

Before anything is built, your work gets mapped and each process is scored on the same three axes. The shortlist falls out of the numbers rather than out of whoever complained loudest.

Axis 01

Volume

How often does it happen, and how long does it take each time? A ten-minute task done forty times a week is a far better target than a two-day task done twice a year.

Axis 02

Judgment

How much real decision-making is involved? Low-judgment, high-volume work automates cleanly. High-judgment work gets assistance, not automation.

Axis 03

Blast radius

What happens when it goes wrong? A mis-sorted email costs a minute. A mis-filed invoice costs money and trust. The score decides how much review gets designed in.

What comes out of it

  • A ranked shortlist of processes, with the reasoning written down
  • An explicit do-not-automate list — the work that should stay human, and why
  • A first build chosen for fastest credible proof, not biggest headline
  • The baseline numbers you'll measure the result against
The arc

What a full engagement looks like.

Most clients start with a single hour and never need this. For the ones who do, the shape is the same every time — and each phase ends with something you can hold.

WEEK 1–2

Map

Shadow the real work, not the org chart version of it. Score every process on volume, judgment and blast radius. You get the ranked shortlist and the do-not-automate list.

WEEK 3–5

Prove

Build one workflow end to end on real work. Measure the hours before and the hours after. If the number doesn't move, we stop and pick differently — that's a success, not a failure.

WEEK 6–10

Extend & teach

Build out the next two or three while your team works alongside. By the end they're building and I'm reviewing, rather than the other way around.

WEEK 11–13

Hand over

Runbooks, a named internal owner, a review rhythm, and documentation another developer could pick up tomorrow. Then either I stay on for administration, or I leave.

When AI Mentor Says No

Work AI Mentor will talk you out of.

Turning down the wrong project is cheaper for both of us than delivering it. These come up often enough to list.

Automating a broken process

If the procedure itself doesn't make sense, automating it just produces the wrong answer faster and makes it harder to fix. The process gets straightened out first — sometimes that's the whole project.

Buying before scoping

Nobody needs another subscription before anyone has established what problem it solves. Most businesses already own most of what they need and aren't using it.

Unsupervised high-stakes decisions

Anything where a confident wrong answer causes real harm — legal, medical, financial filings. Assistance yes, autonomy no, regardless of what the demo showed.

Projects with no owner

If nobody inside the business will own the result after handover, it will be dead within six months. I'd rather find that out in week one than deliver into a vacuum.
